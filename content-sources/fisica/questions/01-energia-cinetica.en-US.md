:::question ec-01
A body has kinetic energy when:
---options---
- it is moving relative to the chosen frame of reference.
- it is at some height above the ground.
- it is attached to a compressed spring.
- it has mass, even when at rest.
---resolution---
Kinetic energy is the **energy of motion**: $KE = \dfrac{m v^2}{2}$. If the speed relative to the frame of reference is zero, $KE = 0$, regardless of the mass.

- Being up high corresponds to **gravitational potential energy**.
- Being attached to a compressed spring corresponds to **elastic potential energy**.
- Having mass is not enough: without speed, there is no kinetic energy.

**Answer:** it is moving relative to the chosen frame of reference.
:::

:::question ec-02
A body of mass $4\ \text{kg}$ moves at $5\ \text{m/s}$. What is its kinetic energy, in joules?
---resolution---
**Formula:** $KE = \dfrac{m v^2}{2}$.

**Substituting:**

$$KE = \frac{4 \times 5^2}{2} = \frac{4 \times 25}{2} = \frac{100}{2} = 50\ \text{J}$$

**Answer:** $50\ \text{J}$.
:::

:::question ec-03
If a body's speed doubles (and its mass does not change), its kinetic energy:
---options---
- also doubles.
- becomes four times larger.
- becomes eight times larger.
- does not change.
---resolution---
Kinetic energy is proportional to the **square** of the speed:

$$\frac{KE_2}{KE_1} = \left(\frac{2v}{v}\right)^2 = 2^2 = 4$$

**Answer:** it becomes four times larger. "Doubles" would be true if we doubled the **mass**, not the speed.
:::

:::question ec-04
An $800\ \text{kg}$ car travels at $90\ \text{km/h}$. What is its kinetic energy, in **kilojoules** (kJ)?
---resolution---
**1. Convert the speed to m/s:**

$$v = \frac{90}{3.6} = 25\ \text{m/s}$$

**2. Calculate the kinetic energy:**

$$KE = \frac{800 \times 25^2}{2} = \frac{800 \times 625}{2} = 250\,000\ \text{J}$$

**3. Convert to kJ:** $250\,000\ \text{J} = 250\ \text{kJ}$.

**Answer:** $250\ \text{kJ}$. Common mistake: plugging $90$ directly into the formula without converting from km/h to m/s.
:::

:::question ec-05
A body's kinetic energy is negative when it moves in the negative direction of the reference axis.
---resolution---
**False.** In the formula $KE = \dfrac{m v^2}{2}$, the speed is **squared**, and $v^2$ is never negative. Mass is also positive. Therefore $KE \ge 0$ always, and the **direction** of motion does not affect kinetic energy. A body moving at $-10\ \text{m/s}$ has the same kinetic energy as at $+10\ \text{m/s}$.
:::

:::question ec-06
Body A has mass $m$ and speed $2v$. Body B has mass $2m$ and speed $v$. The ratio $\dfrac{KE_A}{KE_B}$ of their kinetic energies is:
---options---
- $\dfrac{1}{2}$
- $1$
- $2$
- $4$
---resolution---
$$KE_A = \frac{m\,(2v)^2}{2} = \frac{4 m v^2}{2} = 2 m v^2 \qquad KE_B = \frac{2m\,v^2}{2} = m v^2$$

$$\frac{KE_A}{KE_B} = \frac{2 m v^2}{m v^2} = 2$$

**Answer:** $2$. Twice the speed is "worth" more (a factor of 4) than twice the mass (a factor of 2).
:::

:::question ec-07
A $0.2\ \text{kg}$ ball has $10\ \text{J}$ of kinetic energy. What is its speed, in m/s?
---resolution---
**Solving for $v$:**

$$KE = \frac{m v^2}{2} \quad\Rightarrow\quad v = \sqrt{\frac{2\,KE}{m}}$$

**Substituting:**

$$v = \sqrt{\frac{2 \times 10}{0.2}} = \sqrt{\frac{20}{0.2}} = \sqrt{100} = 10\ \text{m/s}$$

**Answer:** $10\ \text{m/s}$.
:::

:::question ec-08
For a body of constant mass, the graph of kinetic energy versus speed is:
---options---
- a straight line through the origin.
- an arc of a parabola through the origin.
- a hyperbola.
- a horizontal line.
---resolution---
With constant $m$, $KE = \dfrac{m}{2} \cdot v^2$ is a **quadratic** function of $v$, whose graph is a **parabola**. Since $KE = 0$ when $v = 0$, it passes through the origin (and, since speed is positive, we draw only one arc).

A **straight line** through the origin would be the graph of $KE$ versus **mass**, at constant $v$.

**Answer:** an arc of a parabola through the origin.
:::

:::question ec-09
A $2\ \text{kg}$ block, initially at rest, is acted on by a constant net force of $16\ \text{N}$ over $4\ \text{m}$, in the direction of the displacement. What is the block's final speed, in m/s?
---resolution---
**1. Work done by the net force:**

$$W_{\text{net}} = F_{\text{net}} \cdot d = 16 \times 4 = 64\ \text{J}$$

**2. Work–energy theorem** ($KE_i = 0$, since it starts from rest):

$$W_{\text{net}} = \frac{m v_f^2}{2} - 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad 64 = \frac{2 \times v_f^2}{2} = v_f^2$$

$$v_f = \sqrt{64} = 8\ \text{m/s}$$

**Answer:** $8\ \text{m/s}$.
:::

:::question ec-10
A $1\,200\ \text{kg}$ car moving at $20\ \text{m/s}$ brakes and stops after traveling $40\ \text{m}$. What is the magnitude of the average braking force, in newtons?
---resolution---
**1. Initial kinetic energy:**

$$KE_i = \frac{1\,200 \times 20^2}{2} = \frac{1\,200 \times 400}{2} = 240\,000\ \text{J}$$

**2. Work–energy theorem:** the car stops ($KE_f = 0$), and the braking force $F$ opposes the motion ($W = -F d$):

$$-F \times 40 = 0 - 240\,000 \quad\Rightarrow\quad F = \frac{240\,000}{40} = 6\,000\ \text{N}$$

**Answer:** $6\,000\ \text{N}$.
:::

:::question ec-11
A car brakes with a constant friction force and stops after traveling a distance $d$. If the initial speed were three times greater, with the same braking force, the stopping distance would be:
---options---
- $3d$
- $6d$
- $9d$
- $27d$
---resolution---
By the work–energy theorem, all the kinetic energy is removed by the work of friction:

$$f \cdot d = \frac{m v_0^2}{2} \quad\Rightarrow\quad d = \frac{m v_0^2}{2 f}$$

With $m$ and $f$ fixed, $d \propto v_0^2$. Tripling the speed:

$$\frac{d'}{d} = \left(\frac{3 v_0}{v_0}\right)^2 = 9$$

**Answer:** $9d$.
:::

:::question ec-12
What is the net work, in **kilojoules**, needed to accelerate a $1\,000\ \text{kg}$ car from $10\ \text{m/s}$ to $20\ \text{m/s}$?
---resolution---
By the work–energy theorem, $W_{\text{net}} = \Delta KE$:

$$W = \frac{m v_f^2}{2} - \frac{m v_i^2}{2} = \frac{1\,000 \times 400}{2} - \frac{1\,000 \times 100}{2} = 200\,000 - 50\,000 = 150\,000\ \text{J}$$

$150\,000\ \text{J} = 150\ \text{kJ}$.

**Answer:** $150\ \text{kJ}$. Common mistake: computing $\dfrac{m (v_f - v_i)^2}{2} = 50\ \text{kJ}$. The correct expression is the **difference of the squares**, not the square of the difference.
:::

:::question ec-13
A body is in uniform circular motion. About the work done by the net (centripetal) force and the body's kinetic energy, it is correct to say:
---options---
- The work is positive and the kinetic energy increases.
- The work is negative and the kinetic energy decreases.
- The work is zero and the kinetic energy stays constant.
- The work is zero, but the kinetic energy changes.
---resolution---
The centripetal force is always **perpendicular** to the velocity ($\theta = 90°$), so:

$$W = F\,d\cos 90° = 0$$

By the work–energy theorem, $\Delta KE = W_{\text{net}} = 0$: the **speed** does not change (which is why the motion is *uniform*). The centripetal force changes only the **direction** of the velocity.

**Answer:** The work is zero and the kinetic energy stays constant.
:::

:::question ec-14
If a body moves at constant speed, the net work done on it (the sum of the work done by all the forces) is zero.
---resolution---
**True.** By the work–energy theorem, $W_{\text{net}} = \Delta KE$. If the speed is constant, $KE$ does not change and $\Delta KE = 0$, so $W_{\text{net}} = 0$.

This does **not** mean that no force does work: some forces may do positive work and others negative work that cancel out (for example, a block pulled at constant speed against friction).
:::

:::question ec-15
A $2\ \text{kg}$ block slides on a horizontal floor with an initial speed of $6\ \text{m/s}$. The coefficient of kinetic friction between the block and the floor is $0.3$. Use $g = 10\ \text{m/s}^2$. How far, in meters, does the block travel before stopping?
---resolution---
**1. Friction force:**

$$f = \mu\,m\,g = 0.3 \times 2 \times 10 = 6\ \text{N}$$

**2. Initial kinetic energy:**

$$KE_i = \frac{2 \times 6^2}{2} = 36\ \text{J}$$

**3. Work–energy theorem** (only friction does work horizontally):

$$-f\,d = 0 - KE_i \quad\Rightarrow\quad d = \frac{36}{6} = 6\ \text{m}$$

**Checking with the formula** $d = \dfrac{v_0^2}{2\mu g} = \dfrac{36}{2 \times 0.3 \times 10} = \dfrac{36}{6} = 6\ \text{m}$.

**Answer:** $6\ \text{m}$.
:::

:::question ec-16
A $2\ \text{kg}$ body at rest on a frictionless horizontal surface is acted on by a horizontal force that **increases linearly** from $0$ (at $x = 0$) to $20\ \text{N}$ (at $x = 4\ \text{m}$). The body's speed at $x = 4\ \text{m}$ is:
---options---
- $4\ \text{m/s}$
- $\sqrt{40}\ \text{m/s} \approx 6.3\ \text{m/s}$
- $\sqrt{80}\ \text{m/s} \approx 8.9\ \text{m/s}$
- $40\ \text{m/s}$
---resolution---
**1. Work of a variable force = area under the $F \times x$ graph.** The graph is a triangle with base $4\ \text{m}$ and height $20\ \text{N}$:

$$W = \frac{4 \times 20}{2} = 40\ \text{J}$$

**2. Work–energy theorem** (starting from rest, no friction):

$$40 = \frac{2\,v^2}{2} = v^2 \quad\Rightarrow\quad v = \sqrt{40} \approx 6.3\ \text{m/s}$$

The value $\sqrt{80}$ appears if we use $W = F \cdot d = 20 \times 4 = 80\ \text{J}$, which is wrong: the force is not $20\ \text{N}$ over the whole path.

**Answer:** $\sqrt{40}\ \text{m/s} \approx 6.3\ \text{m/s}$.
:::

:::question ec-17
Select **all** the correct statements about kinetic energy.
---options---
- It is a scalar quantity.
- Its value depends on the frame of reference.
- It is directly proportional to speed.
- In SI units, it is measured in joules.
- It can take negative values.
---resolution---
- **It is a scalar:** correct. Energy has no direction.
- **Depends on the frame of reference:** correct. Speed is measured relative to a frame; a passenger sitting still in a bus has $KE = 0$ relative to the bus, but not relative to the street.
- **Proportional to speed:** incorrect. It is proportional to the **square** of the speed.
- **Measured in joules:** correct. $1\ \text{J} = 1\ \text{kg}\cdot\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2$.
- **Can be negative:** incorrect. $m > 0$ and $v^2 \ge 0$, so $KE \ge 0$.
:::

:::question ec-18
A $20\ \text{g}$ bullet moving at $300\ \text{m/s}$ penetrates $15\ \text{cm}$ into a wooden block before stopping. What is the magnitude of the wood's average resisting force, in newtons?
---resolution---
**1. SI units:** $m = 20\ \text{g} = 0.02\ \text{kg}$; $d = 15\ \text{cm} = 0.15\ \text{m}$.

**2. Initial kinetic energy:**

$$KE_i = \frac{0.02 \times 300^2}{2} = \frac{0.02 \times 90\,000}{2} = 900\ \text{J}$$

**3. Work–energy theorem:** the average force $F$ opposes the motion:

$$-F \times 0.15 = 0 - 900 \quad\Rightarrow\quad F = \frac{900}{0.15} = 6\,000\ \text{N}$$

**Answer:** $6\,000\ \text{N}$.
:::

:::question ec-19
A person lifts a box from the floor to a shelf at **constant speed**. About the work done during the lift, it is correct to say:
---options---
- The person's work is zero, since the speed is constant.
- The work done by the weight is positive.
- The net work on the box is zero, because the person's work is positive and the weight's is negative, with the same magnitude.
- The net work on the box is positive and equal to the increase in kinetic energy.
---resolution---
At constant speed, $\Delta KE = 0$; by the work–energy theorem, $W_{\text{net}} = 0$.

- The person pulls up and the box goes up: **positive** work, $W_{\text{person}} = +m g h$.
- The weight points down and the box goes up: **negative** work, $W_g = -m g h$.
- Total: $m g h - m g h = 0$.

The person's work is not zero: it is stored as the box's gravitational potential energy.

**Answer:** The net work on the box is zero, because the person's work is positive and the weight's is negative, with the same magnitude.
:::

:::question ec-20
Match each change (starting from a body with kinetic energy $KE$) to its effect on the kinetic energy.
---pairs---
- Doubling the mass, keeping the speed ||| The kinetic energy doubles
- Doubling the speed, keeping the mass ||| The kinetic energy becomes 4 times larger
- Halving the speed ||| The kinetic energy drops to one quarter
- Tripling the speed ||| The kinetic energy becomes 9 times larger
---resolution---
Use $KE = \dfrac{m v^2}{2}$:

- $KE$ is proportional to $m$: doubling the mass **doubles** the energy.
- $KE$ is proportional to $v^2$:
  - speed $\times 2$ → energy $\times 2^2 = \times 4$;
  - speed $\times \frac{1}{2}$ → energy $\times \frac{1}{4}$;
  - speed $\times 3$ → energy $\times 3^2 = \times 9$.
:::
