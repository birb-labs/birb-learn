:::question gu-01
Two bodies attract each other gravitationally with a force $F$. If the distance between their centers **doubles**, the new force will be:
---options---
- $2F$
- $F/2$
- $F/4$
- $F/8$
---resolution---
The gravitational force is inversely proportional to the **square** of the distance:

$$F' = \frac{G M m}{(2d)^2} = \frac{G M m}{4 d^2} = \frac{F}{4}$$

**Answer:** $F/4$.
:::

:::question gu-02
Two bodies attract each other with a force $F$. If the mass of **each** of them doubles and the distance stays the same, the new force will be:
---options---
- $F$
- $2F$
- $4F$
- $8F$
---resolution---
The force is proportional to the **product** of the masses:

$$F' = \frac{G\,(2M)(2m)}{d^2} = 4\,\frac{G M m}{d^2} = 4F$$

**Answer:** $4F$.
:::

:::question gu-03
The force with which the Earth attracts the Moon is greater than the force with which the Moon attracts the Earth, because the Earth has more mass.
---resolution---
**False.** The two forces form an **action–reaction** pair (Newton's 3rd law): they have the **same magnitude**, $F = \dfrac{G M m}{d^2}$, and opposite directions. What differs are the **effects**: the same force produces a much smaller acceleration on the Earth than on the Moon, because $a = F/m$ and the Earth's mass is much larger.
:::

:::question gu-04
Two bodies attract each other with a force of $36\ \text{N}$. If the distance between them is **tripled**, what will the new force be, in newtons?
---resolution---
$$F' = \frac{F}{3^2} = \frac{36}{9} = 4\ \text{N}$$

**Answer:** $4\ \text{N}$.
:::

:::question gu-05
The approximate value of the universal gravitational constant $G$, in SI units, is:
---options---
- $9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$
- $6.67 \times 10^{-11}\ \text{N}\cdot\text{m}^2/\text{kg}^2$
- $6.67 \times 10^{11}\ \text{N}\cdot\text{m}^2/\text{kg}^2$
- $3.0 \times 10^{8}\ \text{m/s}$
---resolution---
$G \approx 6.67 \times 10^{-11}\ \text{N}\cdot\text{m}^2/\text{kg}^2$. A very small value, which is why the gravitational attraction between everyday objects is imperceptible.

- $9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$ is the acceleration due to gravity $g$ at the Earth's surface, a different quantity.
- $3.0 \times 10^8\ \text{m/s}$ is the speed of light in a vacuum.

**Answer:** $6.67 \times 10^{-11}\ \text{N}\cdot\text{m}^2/\text{kg}^2$.
:::

:::question gu-06
Two bodies attract each other with a force of $5\ \text{N}$. The mass of one of them doubles and the distance between them is halved. What is the new force, in newtons?
---resolution---
$$F' = \frac{G\,(2M)\,m}{(d/2)^2} = \frac{2\,G M m}{d^2/4} = 8\,\frac{G M m}{d^2} = 8F$$

$$F' = 8 \times 5 = 40\ \text{N}$$

**Answer:** $40\ \text{N}$.
:::

:::question gu-07
The Earth's mass is about 81 times the Moon's mass. Let $D$ be the distance between their centers. An object placed on the line segment joining the two centers feels zero net gravitational force at a distance from the Earth's center equal to:
---options---
- $D/2$
- $D/9$
- $0.9\,D$
- $D/81$
---resolution---
Let $x$ be the distance from the Earth's center. The attractions of the Earth and the Moon must have the same magnitude:

$$\frac{G\,(81 m_M)\,m}{x^2} = \frac{G\,m_M\,m}{(D - x)^2} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \frac{81}{x^2} = \frac{1}{(D - x)^2}$$

Taking the square root:

$$\frac{9}{x} = \frac{1}{D - x} \quad\Rightarrow\quad 9D - 9x = x \quad\Rightarrow\quad x = \frac{9D}{10} = 0.9\,D$$

The point is closer to the Moon, which has less mass.

**Answer:** $0.9\,D$.
:::

:::question gu-08
The constant $G$ has the same value anywhere in the Universe, while the acceleration due to gravity $g$ depends on where it is measured.
---resolution---
**True.** $G \approx 6.67 \times 10^{-11}\ \text{N}\cdot\text{m}^2/\text{kg}^2$ is a **universal constant**. On the other hand, $g = \dfrac{G M}{R^2}$ depends on the mass and radius of the celestial body (and on altitude): it is about $9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$ on Earth, $1.6\ \text{m/s}^2$ on the Moon and $3.7\ \text{m/s}^2$ on Mars.
:::

:::question gu-09
The law of universal gravitation was published by:
---options---
- Johannes Kepler, in 1609.
- Galileo Galilei, in 1638.
- Isaac Newton, in 1687.
- Henry Cavendish, in 1798.
---resolution---
Isaac Newton published the law of universal gravitation in the *Philosophiæ Naturalis Principia Mathematica*, in **1687**.

- Kepler published his first two laws in 1609 (*Astronomia Nova*), but they describe the orbits without explaining their cause.
- Cavendish, in 1798, carried out the torsion balance experiment that made it possible to determine $G$ (he measured the Earth's mean density).

**Answer:** Isaac Newton, in 1687.
:::

:::question gu-10
A satellite weighs $2\,000\ \text{N}$ on the Earth's surface. What is the magnitude of the Earth's gravitational force on it, in newtons, when it is at an **altitude** equal to the Earth's radius?
---resolution---
At an altitude equal to the radius $R$, the distance to the Earth's **center** goes from $R$ to $2R$, that is, it **doubles**. Since the force is inversely proportional to the square of the distance:

$$F = \frac{2\,000}{2^2} = \frac{2\,000}{4} = 500\ \text{N}$$

Common mistake: thinking the distance to use is the altitude ($R$), not the distance to the center ($2R$).

**Answer:** $500\ \text{N}$.
:::

:::question gu-11
For a satellite in a circular orbit of radius $r$ around a planet of mass $M$, the orbital speed is $v = \sqrt{GM/r}$. Two satellites, one of $100\ \text{kg}$ and another of $1\,000\ \text{kg}$, are in the same orbit. It is correct to say that:
---options---
- the $1\,000\ \text{kg}$ satellite is 10 times faster.
- the $100\ \text{kg}$ satellite is 10 times faster.
- both have the same speed.
- the heavier satellite slowly falls to lower orbits.
---resolution---
Setting the gravitational force equal to the centripetal net force:

$$\frac{G M m}{r^2} = \frac{m v^2}{r} \quad\Rightarrow\quad v = \sqrt{\frac{G M}{r}}$$

The satellite's mass $m$ **cancels**: the orbital speed depends only on the planet's mass and the radius of the orbit.

**Answer:** both have the same speed.
:::

:::question gu-12
About Henry Cavendish's experiment, select **all** the correct statements.
---options---
- He used a torsion balance to measure the attraction between lead spheres.
- It was carried out in 1798.
- It made it possible to determine the Earth's mean density, which is equivalent to determining the constant $G$.
- It was done with a telescope, by observing the Moon's orbit.
---resolution---
- **Torsion balance:** correct. The balance had been designed by John Michell, and Cavendish used it to measure the tiny attraction between lead spheres.
- **1798:** correct.
- **Earth's density / $G$:** correct. Cavendish's stated goal was the Earth's mean density (which he found to be about 5.5 times that of water), and that result is equivalent to knowing $G$.
- **Telescope:** incorrect. It was a laboratory experiment.
:::
