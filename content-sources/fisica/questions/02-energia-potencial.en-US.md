:::question ep-01
A $3\ \text{kg}$ flowerpot sits on a wall $4\ \text{m}$ high. Taking the ground as the reference level and $g = 10\ \text{m/s}^2$, what is the flowerpot's gravitational potential energy, in joules?
---resolution---
$$PE_g = m\,g\,h = 3 \times 10 \times 4 = 120\ \text{J}$$

**Answer:** $120\ \text{J}$.
:::

:::question ep-02
Which of the forces below is **conservative**?
---options---
- Kinetic friction.
- Air resistance.
- Weight.
- The muscular force of a person pushing a cart.
---resolution---
A force is conservative when its work between two points **does not depend on the path** (and is zero around a closed path). **Weight** has this property: $W_g = m g (h_i - h_f)$ depends only on the initial and final heights.

Friction and air resistance depend on the path (the longer it is, the more energy they dissipate), and muscular force depends on how the person acts. They are non-conservative.

**Answer:** Weight.
:::

:::question ep-03
The work done by a body's weight depends on the path taken between the initial and final points.
---resolution---
**False.** Weight is a **conservative** force: its work depends only on the height difference between the initial and final points,

$$W_g = m\,g\,(h_i - h_f),$$

whether the body went straight down, along a ramp or down a staircase.
:::

:::question ep-04
A $2\ \text{kg}$ ball falls from a height of $12\ \text{m}$ to a height of $5\ \text{m}$. Use $g = 10\ \text{m/s}^2$. How much work does the weight do over this stretch, in joules?
---resolution---
By the potential energy theorem (or directly from the formula for the work done by weight):

$$W_g = m\,g\,(h_i - h_f) = 2 \times 10 \times (12 - 5) = 2 \times 10 \times 7 = 140\ \text{J}$$

The work is **positive** because the ball went down (the weight points in the same direction as the vertical displacement).

**Answer:** $140\ \text{J}$.
:::

:::question ep-05
A $5\ \text{kg}$ block slides down a $10\ \text{m}$-long ramp whose top is $6\ \text{m}$ above its bottom. With $g = 10\ \text{m/s}^2$, the work done by the weight over the whole descent is:
---options---
- $300\ \text{J}$
- $400\ \text{J}$
- $500\ \text{J}$
- $800\ \text{J}$
---resolution---
The work done by weight depends only on the **height difference**, not on the length of the ramp:

$$W_g = m\,g\,h = 5 \times 10 \times 6 = 300\ \text{J}$$

The value $500\ \text{J}$ appears if we mistakenly use the length of the ramp ($10\ \text{m}$) instead of the height.

**Answer:** $300\ \text{J}$.
:::

:::question ep-06
A spring with spring constant $k = 500\ \text{N/m}$ is compressed by $20\ \text{cm}$. What is the stored elastic potential energy, in joules?
---resolution---
**Convert:** $x = 20\ \text{cm} = 0.2\ \text{m}$.

$$PE_{el} = \frac{k\,x^2}{2} = \frac{500 \times 0.2^2}{2} = \frac{500 \times 0.04}{2} = \frac{20}{2} = 10\ \text{J}$$

**Answer:** $10\ \text{J}$.
:::

:::question ep-07
If the deformation of an ideal spring doubles, the stored elastic potential energy:
---options---
- doubles.
- becomes three times larger.
- becomes four times larger.
- does not change, since the spring constant is the same.
---resolution---
$PE_{el} = \dfrac{k x^2}{2}$ is proportional to the **square** of the deformation:

$$\frac{E_2}{E_1} = \left(\frac{2x}{x}\right)^2 = 4$$

It is the elastic **force** ($F = kx$) that would double.

**Answer:** becomes four times larger.
:::

:::question ep-08
An ideal spring exerts a force of $40\ \text{N}$ when deformed by $8\ \text{cm}$. What is its spring constant, in N/m?
---resolution---
**Hooke's law:** $F = k\,x$, with $x = 8\ \text{cm} = 0.08\ \text{m}$.

$$k = \frac{F}{x} = \frac{40}{0.08} = 500\ \text{N/m}$$

**Answer:** $500\ \text{N/m}$.
:::

:::question ep-09
The potential energy theorem states that the work done by a conservative force equals:
---options---
- the change in the body's kinetic energy.
- the change in potential energy, $\Delta PE = PE_f - PE_i$.
- minus the change in potential energy, $-\Delta PE = PE_i - PE_f$.
- the total mechanical energy of the system.
---resolution---
**Potential energy theorem:**

$$W_{\text{conservative}} = -\Delta PE = PE_i - PE_f$$

When the conservative force does positive work (a body falling), the potential energy **decreases**; when it does negative work (a body rising), the potential energy **increases**.

The first option describes the **work–energy theorem**, which uses the **net** work.

**Answer:** minus the change in potential energy, $-\Delta PE = PE_i - PE_f$.
:::

:::question ep-10
A $0.5\ \text{kg}$ book is on a shelf $2.0\ \text{m}$ above the floor. A table in the same room is $0.8\ \text{m}$ high. Use $g = 10\ \text{m/s}^2$. What is the book's gravitational potential energy, in joules, taking the **tabletop** as the reference level?
---resolution---
**Height relative to the table:**

$$h = 2.0 - 0.8 = 1.2\ \text{m}$$

$$PE_g = m\,g\,h = 0.5 \times 10 \times 1.2 = 6\ \text{J}$$

Relative to the floor, it would be $0.5 \times 10 \times 2 = 10\ \text{J}$: the value depends on the chosen reference.

**Answer:** $6\ \text{J}$.
:::

:::question ep-11
A body's gravitational potential energy can be negative.
---resolution---
**True.** In $PE_g = m g h$, the height $h$ is measured from a chosen **reference level**. If the body is **below** that level, $h < 0$ and $PE_g < 0$. For example, an object at the bottom of a well has negative potential energy if the reference is the surface. This is not a physical problem: only **changes** in potential energy have meaning, and they do not depend on the reference.
:::

:::question ep-12
A uniform rod $2\ \text{m}$ long with a mass of $6\ \text{kg}$ lies on the ground. With $g = 10\ \text{m/s}^2$, the minimum work needed to stand it upright, vertically, is:
---options---
- $30\ \text{J}$
- $60\ \text{J}$
- $120\ \text{J}$
- $240\ \text{J}$
---resolution---
The minimum work equals the increase in gravitational potential energy. For an extended body, we use the height of the **center of mass**, which in a uniform rod is at the middle:

$$\Delta h_{cm} = \frac{2}{2} = 1\ \text{m}$$

$$W_{\text{min}} = m\,g\,\Delta h_{cm} = 6 \times 10 \times 1 = 60\ \text{J}$$

The value $120\ \text{J}$ appears if we use the full length of the rod (only the tip rises 2 m).

**Answer:** $60\ \text{J}$.
:::

:::question ep-13
The graph of a spring's elastic force versus deformation is a straight line through the origin and the point $(0.2\ \text{m};\ 30\ \text{N})$. What is the stored elastic potential energy, in joules, when the deformation is $0.2\ \text{m}$?
---resolution---
The stored energy is the **area under the** $F \times x$ **graph**, which here is a triangle:

$$PE_{el} = \frac{\text{base} \times \text{height}}{2} = \frac{0.2 \times 30}{2} = 3\ \text{J}$$

**Check:** $k = \dfrac{30}{0.2} = 150\ \text{N/m}$ and $PE_{el} = \dfrac{150 \times 0.2^2}{2} = \dfrac{150 \times 0.04}{2} = 3\ \text{J}$.

**Answer:** $3\ \text{J}$.
:::

:::question ep-14
Select **all** the **non-conservative** forces.
---options---
- Kinetic friction between a block and the floor.
- Air resistance on a skydiver.
- The weight of a stone.
- The elastic force of an ideal spring.
---resolution---
- **Kinetic friction** and **air resistance** are non-conservative (dissipative): their work depends on the path and, around a closed path, is negative. They turn mechanical energy into thermal energy.
- **Weight** and the **elastic force** of an ideal spring are conservative: the work depends only on the initial and final positions, and each has an associated potential energy ($m g h$ and $\frac{k x^2}{2}$).
:::

:::question ep-15
A spring with constant $k = 400\ \text{N/m}$ is already compressed by $10\ \text{cm}$. How much work, in joules, is needed to increase the compression to $30\ \text{cm}$?
---resolution---
The work needed is the **change** in elastic energy between the two deformations:

$$E_1 = \frac{400 \times 0.1^2}{2} = \frac{400 \times 0.01}{2} = 2\ \text{J} \qquad E_2 = \frac{400 \times 0.3^2}{2} = \frac{400 \times 0.09}{2} = 18\ \text{J}$$

$$W = E_2 - E_1 = 18 - 2 = 16\ \text{J}$$

Common mistake: calculating the energy of a $20\ \text{cm}$ deformation ($\frac{400 \times 0.04}{2} = 8\ \text{J}$). Since the energy depends on $x^2$, we cannot simply subtract the deformations.

**Answer:** $16\ \text{J}$.
:::

:::question ep-16
A box is dragged across a horizontal floor from point A to point B and then back to A. About the net work done along this closed path, it is correct to say:
---options---
- The work done by weight is negative and the work done by friction is zero.
- The work done by weight is zero and the work done by friction is negative.
- The work done by weight and by friction are both zero.
- The work done by weight and by friction are both negative.
---resolution---
- **Weight:** a conservative force. Around a closed path, its work is **zero** (here, in fact, the displacement is horizontal and the weight does no work on any stretch).
- **Friction:** a dissipative force. It opposes the motion **both ways**, so it does negative work on both stretches, and the total is **negative**.

This difference is exactly what classifies a force as conservative or not.

**Answer:** The work done by weight is zero and the work done by friction is negative.
:::

:::question ep-17
On the Moon, where $g = 1.6\ \text{m/s}^2$, an astronaut lifts a $10\ \text{kg}$ box to a height of $3\ \text{m}$. What is the increase in the box's gravitational potential energy, in joules?
---resolution---
$$\Delta PE_g = m\,g\,\Delta h = 10 \times 1.6 \times 3 = 48\ \text{J}$$

On Earth (with $g = 10\ \text{m/s}^2$), the same lift would take $300\ \text{J}$.

**Answer:** $48\ \text{J}$.
:::

:::question ep-18
A $3\ \text{kg}$ body is hung from a vertical spring, which stretches $15\ \text{cm}$ until equilibrium. With $g = 10\ \text{m/s}^2$, the elastic potential energy stored in the spring at equilibrium is:
---options---
- $0.45\ \text{J}$
- $2.25\ \text{J}$
- $4.5\ \text{J}$
- $22.5\ \text{J}$
---resolution---
**1. Spring constant**, from equilibrium ($k x = m g$):

$$k = \frac{m g}{x} = \frac{3 \times 10}{0.15} = 200\ \text{N/m}$$

**2. Stored energy:**

$$PE_{el} = \frac{k x^2}{2} = \frac{200 \times 0.15^2}{2} = \frac{200 \times 0.0225}{2} = 2.25\ \text{J}$$

The value $4.5\ \text{J}$ appears if we forget the factor $\frac{1}{2}$.

**Answer:** $2.25\ \text{J}$.
:::

:::question ep-19
Changing the reference level changes the **change** in a body's gravitational potential energy between two points.
---resolution---
**False.** Changing the reference changes the **values** of $PE_g$ at each point (adding the same constant to all of them), but the **change** between two points,

$$\Delta PE_g = m\,g\,(h_f - h_i),$$

depends only on the height difference between them, which is the same for any reference.
:::

:::question ep-20
Order the bodies from **lowest** to **highest** gravitational potential energy relative to the ground ($g = 10\ \text{m/s}^2$).
---options---
- A $5\ \text{kg}$ body at a height of $1\ \text{m}$
- A $2\ \text{kg}$ body at a height of $3\ \text{m}$
- A $1\ \text{kg}$ body at a height of $10\ \text{m}$
- A $0.5\ \text{kg}$ body at a height of $30\ \text{m}$
---resolution---
Calculating $PE_g = m g h$ for each:

- $5\ \text{kg}$ at $1\ \text{m}$: $5 \times 10 \times 1 = 50\ \text{J}$
- $2\ \text{kg}$ at $3\ \text{m}$: $2 \times 10 \times 3 = 60\ \text{J}$
- $1\ \text{kg}$ at $10\ \text{m}$: $1 \times 10 \times 10 = 100\ \text{J}$
- $0.5\ \text{kg}$ at $30\ \text{m}$: $0.5 \times 10 \times 30 = 150\ \text{J}$

Increasing order: $50 < 60 < 100 < 150$. Notice that the heaviest body has the **lowest** potential energy: what matters is the product $m \cdot h$.
:::
