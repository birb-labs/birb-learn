:::question gr-01
An astronaut travels from the Earth to the Moon. About their mass and weight, it is correct to say:
---options---
- Both mass and weight decrease.
- The mass stays the same and the weight decreases.
- The mass decreases and the weight stays the same.
- Both mass and weight stay the same.
---resolution---
- **Mass** is the amount of matter (and the measure of inertia): it **does not change** from place to place.
- **Weight** is the gravitational force, $W = m\,g$. Since $g$ on the Moon (about $1.6\ \text{m/s}^2$) is much smaller than on Earth (about $9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$), the weight **decreases** (to about one sixth).

**Answer:** The mass stays the same and the weight decreases.
:::

:::question gr-02
What is the weight, in newtons, of a $70\ \text{kg}$ astronaut on the surface of the Moon, where $g = 1.6\ \text{m/s}^2$?
---resolution---
$$W = m\,g = 70 \times 1.6 = 112\ \text{N}$$

On Earth (with $g = 10\ \text{m/s}^2$), the weight would be $700\ \text{N}$. The mass is still $70\ \text{kg}$ in both places.

**Answer:** $112\ \text{N}$.
:::

:::question gr-03
At the Earth's surface, $g = 10\ \text{m/s}^2$. What is the value of $g$, in m/s², at an **altitude** equal to the Earth's radius?
---resolution---
$$g_h = \frac{G M}{(R + h)^2}$$

With $h = R$, the distance to the center is $2R$:

$$g_h = \frac{G M}{(2R)^2} = \frac{1}{4} \cdot \frac{G M}{R^2} = \frac{g_0}{4} = \frac{10}{4} = 2.5\ \text{m/s}^2$$

**Answer:** $2.5\ \text{m/s}^2$.
:::

:::question gr-04
A planet has twice the Earth's mass and twice the Earth's radius. The acceleration due to gravity at the surface of this planet, compared with Earth's ($g$), is:
---options---
- $2g$
- $g$
- $g/2$
- $g/4$
---resolution---
$$g = \frac{G M}{R^2} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \frac{g_P}{g_E} = \frac{M_P}{M_E} \cdot \left(\frac{R_E}{R_P}\right)^2 = 2 \times \frac{1}{4} = \frac{1}{2}$$

The doubled mass multiplies $g$ by 2, but the doubled radius divides it by 4.

**Answer:** $g/2$.
:::

:::question gr-05
Astronauts float inside the International Space Station because there is no gravity at the altitude where it orbits.
---resolution---
**False.** At the ISS's altitude (about 400 km), the acceleration due to gravity is roughly $8.7\ \text{m/s}^2$, about 90% of its surface value. The astronauts float because they and the station are **in free fall** together around the Earth: they all fall with the same acceleration, so nothing presses them against the "floor". This state is called **weightlessness** (or microgravity).
:::

:::question gr-06
Take the Earth's radius to be $6\,400\ \text{km}$. At what **altitude**, in kilometers, is the acceleration due to gravity equal to $\frac{1}{16}$ of its surface value?
---resolution---
$$\frac{g_h}{g_0} = \left(\frac{R}{R + h}\right)^2 = \frac{1}{16} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \frac{R}{R + h} = \frac{1}{4} \quad\Rightarrow\quad R + h = 4R$$

$$h = 3R = 3 \times 6\,400 = 19\,200\ \text{km}$$

Common mistake: answering $4R = 25\,600\ \text{km}$, which is the distance to the **center**, not the altitude.

**Answer:** $19\,200\ \text{km}$.
:::

:::question gr-07
On the surface of the Moon, where there is no atmosphere, a hammer and a feather are dropped at the same time from the same height. It is correct to say that:
---options---
- the hammer lands first, because it is heavier.
- the feather lands first, because it is lighter.
- both land at the same time.
- both float, because there is no gravity on the Moon.
---resolution---
Without air resistance, all bodies fall with the **same acceleration** $g = \dfrac{G M}{R^2}$, which does not depend on the mass of the falling body. That is why they land together. This experiment was actually carried out by astronaut David Scott, on the Apollo 15 mission, in 1971.

**Answer:** both land at the same time.
:::

:::question gr-08
A planet has 8 times the Earth's mass and 2 times its radius. On Earth, $g = 10\ \text{m/s}^2$. What would a $50\ \text{kg}$ person weigh, in newtons, on the surface of this planet?
---resolution---
**1. Gravity on the planet:**

$$\frac{g_P}{g_E} = \frac{M_P}{M_E} \cdot \left(\frac{R_E}{R_P}\right)^2 = 8 \times \frac{1}{4} = 2 \quad\Rightarrow\quad g_P = 2 \times 10 = 20\ \text{m/s}^2$$

**2. Weight:**

$$W = m\,g_P = 50 \times 20 = 1\,000\ \text{N}$$

**Answer:** $1\,000\ \text{N}$ (twice the weight on Earth).
:::

:::question gr-09
Two satellites move in circular orbits around the Earth: satellite A, of radius $r$, and satellite B, of radius $4r$. Given that $v = \sqrt{GM/r}$, B's orbital speed, relative to A's ($v_A$), is:
---options---
- $4\,v_A$
- $2\,v_A$
- $v_A / 2$
- $v_A / 4$
---resolution---
$$\frac{v_B}{v_A} = \sqrt{\frac{G M / (4r)}{G M / r}} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{4}} = \frac{1}{2}$$

Higher orbits are **slower**.

**Answer:** $v_A / 2$.
:::

:::question gr-10
The acceleration due to gravity at a point above a planet's surface depends on which factors? Select **all** that apply.
---options---
- The planet's mass.
- The distance from the point to the planet's center.
- The mass of the falling body.
- The altitude of the point.
---resolution---
$$g = \frac{G M}{(R + h)^2}$$

- **The planet's mass ($M$):** yes.
- **Distance to the center ($R + h$):** yes.
- **Altitude ($h$):** yes, since it changes the distance to the center.
- **Mass of the falling body:** no. It cancels when we set $m g$ equal to $\dfrac{G M m}{d^2}$.
:::

:::question gr-11
At the Earth's surface, the value of $g$ at the Equator is slightly smaller than at the poles.
---resolution---
**True.** $g$ is about $9.78\ \text{m/s}^2$ at the Equator and about $9.83\ \text{m/s}^2$ at the poles. Two factors contribute: the Earth is slightly flattened (at the Equator the surface is farther from the center) and the Earth's rotation reduces the apparent weight, an effect that is greatest at the Equator.
:::

:::question gr-12
A geostationary satellite always stays above the same point on the Equator. For that, its orbital period must be:
---options---
- about 90 minutes, like that of the International Space Station.
- about 12 hours, like that of GPS satellites.
- equal to the Earth's rotation period (about 24 hours).
- about 27 days, like that of the Moon.
---resolution---
To stay fixed relative to the ground, the satellite has to go around **together with the Earth**: the same period as the Earth's rotation (one sidereal day, about 23 h 56 min), in the plane of the Equator and in the same direction. By Kepler's 3rd law, this period corresponds to an orbit with a radius of about $42\,000\ \text{km}$ (altitude of roughly $36\,000\ \text{km}$).

**Answer:** equal to the Earth's rotation period (about 24 hours).
:::
