:::question em-01
A stone is dropped from rest from a height of $45\ \text{m}$. Ignoring air resistance and using $g = 10\ \text{m/s}^2$, with what speed, in m/s, does it hit the ground?
---resolution---
Only the weight does work: mechanical energy is conserved. With the reference at the ground:

$$m\,g\,h = \frac{m v^2}{2} \quad\Rightarrow\quad v = \sqrt{2 g h} = \sqrt{2 \times 10 \times 45} = \sqrt{900} = 30\ \text{m/s}$$

**Answer:** $30\ \text{m/s}$.
:::

:::question em-02
The mechanical energy of a system is:
---options---
- the kinetic energy minus the potential energy.
- the sum of the kinetic energy and the potential energy (gravitational and elastic).
- the product of the kinetic energy and the potential energy.
- only the gravitational potential energy.
---resolution---
By definition:

$$ME = KE + PE = \frac{m v^2}{2} + m g h + \frac{k x^2}{2}$$

(including only the terms present in the situation).

**Answer:** the sum of the kinetic energy and the potential energy (gravitational and elastic).
:::

:::question em-03
A pendulum is released from rest at a point $80\ \text{cm}$ above the lowest point of its path. Ignoring air resistance ($g = 10\ \text{m/s}^2$), what is the speed, in m/s, at the lowest point?
---resolution---
The string tension is perpendicular to the velocity and does no work; only the weight does work. Conservation of mechanical energy, with the reference at the lowest point and $h = 0.8\ \text{m}$:

$$m g h = \frac{m v^2}{2} \quad\Rightarrow\quad v = \sqrt{2 g h} = \sqrt{2 \times 10 \times 0.8} = \sqrt{16} = 4\ \text{m/s}$$

**Answer:** $4\ \text{m/s}$.
:::

:::question em-04
A ball is thrown straight up at $20\ \text{m/s}$. Ignoring air resistance and using $g = 10\ \text{m/s}^2$, the maximum height reached, relative to the launch point, is:
---options---
- $10\ \text{m}$
- $20\ \text{m}$
- $40\ \text{m}$
- $200\ \text{m}$
---resolution---
At the highest point, the speed is zero: all the kinetic energy has become potential energy.

$$\frac{m v_0^2}{2} = m g h \quad\Rightarrow\quad h = \frac{v_0^2}{2 g} = \frac{20^2}{2 \times 10} = \frac{400}{20} = 20\ \text{m}$$

**Answer:** $20\ \text{m}$.
:::

:::question em-05
A roller coaster car starts from rest at a point $30\ \text{m}$ high. Ignoring friction ($g = 10\ \text{m/s}^2$), what is its speed, in m/s, as it passes a point $10\ \text{m}$ high?
---resolution---
Conservation of mechanical energy, with the reference at the ground:

$$m g h_i = \frac{m v^2}{2} + m g h_f \quad\Rightarrow\quad v^2 = 2 g (h_i - h_f) = 2 \times 10 \times 20 = 400$$

$$v = 20\ \text{m/s}$$

The shape of the track between the two points does not matter: only the height difference.

**Answer:** $20\ \text{m/s}$.
:::

:::question em-06
In a conservative system, the mechanical energy stays constant.
---resolution---
**True.** In a conservative system, only conservative forces (weight, elastic force) do work. By the mechanical energy theorem, $\Delta ME = W_{\text{non-cons}} = 0$, that is, $ME_i = ME_f$: energy only changes between kinetic and potential.
:::

:::question em-07
Two identical blocks start from rest at the top of two **frictionless** ramps of the same height: ramp A is gentle and long; ramp B is steep and short. When they reach the bottom:
---options---
- the block on ramp A arrives faster, since it traveled a longer path.
- the block on ramp B arrives faster, since the ramp is steeper.
- both arrive with the same speed.
- it is impossible to compare without knowing the angles.
---resolution---
Without friction, mechanical energy is conserved, and the speed at the bottom depends only on the height difference:

$$v = \sqrt{2 g h}$$

Both ramps have the same height $h$, so the final speeds are **equal**. What changes is the **time** of descent (the steep ramp is covered more quickly).

**Answer:** both arrive with the same speed.
:::

:::question em-08
A spring with constant $k = 800\ \text{N/m}$, compressed by $50\ \text{cm}$, launches a $2\ \text{kg}$ block horizontally across a frictionless surface. What is the block's speed, in m/s, after it leaves the spring?
---resolution---
**Initial elastic energy:**

$$PE_{el} = \frac{k x^2}{2} = \frac{800 \times 0.5^2}{2} = \frac{800 \times 0.25}{2} = 100\ \text{J}$$

**All of it becomes kinetic energy** (horizontal, frictionless surface):

$$\frac{m v^2}{2} = 100 \quad\Rightarrow\quad v^2 = \frac{2 \times 100}{2} = 100 \quad\Rightarrow\quad v = 10\ \text{m/s}$$

**Answer:** $10\ \text{m/s}$.
:::

:::question em-09
A $0.5\ \text{kg}$ block compresses a spring with constant $k = 1\,000\ \text{N/m}$ by $20\ \text{cm}$. Once released, it travels along a frictionless track and goes up a ramp. Use $g = 10\ \text{m/s}^2$. What maximum height, in meters, does the block reach relative to its starting level?
---resolution---
**Initial energy** (elastic only):

$$PE_{el} = \frac{1\,000 \times 0.2^2}{2} = \frac{1\,000 \times 0.04}{2} = 20\ \text{J}$$

**At the highest point**, the speed is zero and the energy is entirely gravitational:

$$m g h = 20 \quad\Rightarrow\quad h = \frac{20}{0.5 \times 10} = \frac{20}{5} = 4\ \text{m}$$

**Answer:** $4\ \text{m}$.
:::

:::question em-10
A $50\ \text{kg}$ skier starts from rest at the top of a $10\ \text{m}$-high slope and reaches the bottom at $10\ \text{m/s}$. With $g = 10\ \text{m/s}^2$, the energy dissipated on the way down was:
---options---
- $0$
- $2\,500\ \text{J}$
- $5\,000\ \text{J}$
- $7\,500\ \text{J}$
---resolution---
Reference at the bottom.

$$ME_i = m g h = 50 \times 10 \times 10 = 5\,000\ \text{J} \qquad ME_f = \frac{m v^2}{2} = \frac{50 \times 100}{2} = 2\,500\ \text{J}$$

$$E_{\text{dissipated}} = ME_i - ME_f = 5\,000 - 2\,500 = 2\,500\ \text{J}$$

**Answer:** $2\,500\ \text{J}$ (half of the initial mechanical energy).
:::

:::question em-11
A $2\ \text{kg}$ block slides at $10\ \text{m/s}$ across a rough horizontal floor until it stops. How much energy, in joules, was dissipated by friction?
---resolution---
On the horizontal floor, the potential energy does not change. All the initial kinetic energy is dissipated:

$$E_{\text{dissipated}} = KE_i - 0 = \frac{2 \times 10^2}{2} = 100\ \text{J}$$

**Answer:** $100\ \text{J}$ (converted mainly into thermal energy).
:::

:::question em-12
A small sphere starts from rest and slides without friction along a track that ends in a vertical loop of radius $R = 2\ \text{m}$. The minimum starting height, measured from the bottom of the loop, for the sphere to complete the loop without losing contact with the track is:
---options---
- $2\ \text{m}$
- $4\ \text{m}$
- $5\ \text{m}$
- $6\ \text{m}$
---resolution---
**At the top of the loop**, the minimum speed occurs when the normal force is zero and the weight alone is the centripetal force:

$$m g = \frac{m v^2}{R} \quad\Rightarrow\quad v^2 = g R$$

**Conservation of energy** between the start (height $h$) and the top (height $2R$):

$$m g h = \frac{m v^2}{2} + m g (2R) = \frac{m g R}{2} + 2 m g R \quad\Rightarrow\quad h = \frac{5R}{2}$$

$$h = \frac{5 \times 2}{2} = 5\ \text{m}$$

The value $4\ \text{m}$ ($= 2R$) would be enough for the sphere to reach the top with zero speed, but it would then fall before completing the loop.

**Answer:** $5\ \text{m}$.
:::

:::question em-13
If a body's speed stays constant, its mechanical energy is necessarily constant too.
---resolution---
**False.** Constant speed guarantees that the **kinetic** energy is constant, but the **potential** energy can change. Examples:

- a skydiver descending at terminal velocity: constant $KE$, decreasing $PE$, so $ME$ **decreases**;
- an elevator rising at constant speed: constant $KE$, increasing $PE$, so $ME$ **increases**.
:::

:::question em-14
A $4\ \text{kg}$ block is released from the top of a ramp with friction, $5\ \text{m}$ above its bottom, and reaches the bottom at $8\ \text{m/s}$. Use $g = 10\ \text{m/s}^2$. How much energy, in joules, was dissipated by friction?
---resolution---
Reference at the bottom.

**Initial mechanical energy** (at rest at the top):

$$ME_i = m g h = 4 \times 10 \times 5 = 200\ \text{J}$$

**Final mechanical energy** (at the bottom):

$$ME_f = \frac{m v^2}{2} = \frac{4 \times 8^2}{2} = \frac{4 \times 64}{2} = 128\ \text{J}$$

**Dissipated energy:**

$$E_{\text{diss}} = 200 - 128 = 72\ \text{J}$$

Equivalently, the work done by friction was $W_f = \Delta ME = -72\ \text{J}$.

**Answer:** $72\ \text{J}$.
:::

:::question em-15
A skydiver descends with the parachute open, at constant speed. During this descent:
---options---
- the kinetic energy decreases and the mechanical energy is constant.
- the potential energy is constant and the mechanical energy decreases.
- the kinetic energy is constant, the potential energy decreases and the mechanical energy decreases.
- the kinetic, potential and mechanical energies are all constant.
---resolution---
- **Constant speed** → **constant kinetic** energy.
- **Decreasing height** → **potential** energy **decreases**.
- $ME = KE + PE$ → **mechanical** energy **decreases**.

The lost mechanical energy is dissipated by air resistance (a non-conservative force), which does negative work equal to $-m g \Delta h$.

**Answer:** the kinetic energy is constant, the potential energy decreases and the mechanical energy decreases.
:::

:::question em-16
A $0.5\ \text{kg}$ ball is dropped from a height of $3\ \text{m}$ and, after hitting the floor, rises to $1.8\ \text{m}$. Use $g = 10\ \text{m/s}^2$. How much mechanical energy, in joules, was dissipated in the collision with the floor?
---resolution---
At the highest points the speed is zero, so the mechanical energy is potential only.

$$ME_i = 0.5 \times 10 \times 3 = 15\ \text{J} \qquad ME_f = 0.5 \times 10 \times 1.8 = 9\ \text{J}$$

$$E_{\text{diss}} = 15 - 9 = 6\ \text{J}$$

**Answer:** $6\ \text{J}$ (40% of the initial energy).
:::

:::question em-17
In which situations (idealized as described) is mechanical energy **conserved**? Select all that apply.
---options---
- A stone in free fall in a vacuum.
- A pendulum swinging without friction or air resistance.
- A car braking to a stop on a flat road.
- An elevator being raised by a motor at constant speed.
- A block sliding down a perfectly smooth ramp.
---resolution---
Mechanical energy is conserved when **only conservative forces do work**.

- **Free fall in a vacuum:** only the weight acts. Conserved.
- **Ideal pendulum:** the tension is perpendicular to the motion (zero work); only the weight does work. Conserved.
- **Car braking:** friction does negative work. Mechanical energy **decreases**.
- **Elevator with a motor:** the motor does positive work. Mechanical energy **increases**.
- **Smooth ramp:** the normal force is perpendicular to the motion; only the weight does work. Conserved.
:::

:::question em-18
From the top of a $20\ \text{m}$ building, a stone is thrown **horizontally** at $10\ \text{m/s}$. Ignoring air resistance ($g = 10\ \text{m/s}^2$), the speed with which the stone hits the ground is:
---options---
- $10\ \text{m/s}$
- $20\ \text{m/s}$
- $10\sqrt{5}\ \text{m/s} \approx 22.4\ \text{m/s}$
- $30\ \text{m/s}$
---resolution---
Kinetic energy is a scalar: the direction of the throw does not matter. Conservation of energy, with the reference at the ground:

$$\frac{m v_0^2}{2} + m g h = \frac{m v^2}{2} \quad\Rightarrow\quad v^2 = v_0^2 + 2 g h = 100 + 2 \times 10 \times 20 = 500$$

$$v = \sqrt{500} = \sqrt{100 \times 5} = 10\sqrt{5} \approx 22.4\ \text{m/s}$$

The value $30\ \text{m/s}$ appears if we add the speeds ($10 + 20$), which is wrong: it is the **energies** that add, not the speeds.

**Answer:** $10\sqrt{5}\ \text{m/s} \approx 22.4\ \text{m/s}$.
:::

:::question em-19
A $1\ \text{kg}$ block is dropped from rest $1.8\ \text{m}$ above the top of a vertical spring with constant $k = 1\,000\ \text{N/m}$. Ignoring friction and using $g = 10\ \text{m/s}^2$, what is the maximum compression of the spring, in **meters**?
---resolution---
**States:** the start (at rest, $1.8\ \text{m}$ above the spring) and maximum compression $x$ (at rest again). Between them, the block drops $1.8 + x$ in total. With the reference at the point of maximum compression:

$$m g (1.8 + x) = \frac{k x^2}{2} \quad\Rightarrow\quad 10\,(1.8 + x) = 500\,x^2$$

$$500\,x^2 - 10\,x - 18 = 0$$

**Quadratic formula:**

$$x = \frac{10 \pm \sqrt{100 + 4 \times 500 \times 18}}{2 \times 500} = \frac{10 \pm \sqrt{36\,100}}{1\,000} = \frac{10 \pm 190}{1\,000}$$

The positive root is $x = \dfrac{200}{1\,000} = 0.2\ \text{m}$.

**Check:** potential energy lost $= 1 \times 10 \times 2 = 20\ \text{J}$; elastic energy $= \frac{1\,000 \times 0.04}{2} = 20\ \text{J}$. ✓

**Answer:** $0.2\ \text{m}$ ($20\ \text{cm}$).
:::

:::question em-20
A motor raises an $800\ \text{kg}$ elevator $15\ \text{m}$ at constant speed. Ignoring friction ($g = 10\ \text{m/s}^2$), the work done by the motor is:
---options---
- $0$, since the speed is constant.
- $12\,000\ \text{J}$
- $120\,000\ \text{J}$
- $1\,200\,000\ \text{J}$
---resolution---
The motor's force is non-conservative, so $W_{\text{motor}} = \Delta ME$. At constant speed, $\Delta KE = 0$, and:

$$W_{\text{motor}} = \Delta PE = m g \Delta h = 800 \times 10 \times 15 = 120\,000\ \text{J}$$

It is the **net** work (motor + weight) that is zero, not the motor's work.

**Answer:** $120\,000\ \text{J}$.
:::

:::question em-21
Match each situation to what happens to the mechanical energy.
---pairs---
- An ideal pendulum swinging in a vacuum ||| Conservative system: the mechanical energy is constant
- A car braking to a stop on a flat road ||| Dissipative system: the mechanical energy decreases
- An elevator rising, pulled by a motor ||| The mechanical energy increases thanks to the motor's work
- A skydiver at terminal velocity ||| The kinetic energy is constant, but the mechanical energy decreases
---resolution---
Use the mechanical energy theorem, $W_{\text{non-cons}} = \Delta ME$:

- **Ideal pendulum:** no non-conservative force does work, so $\Delta ME = 0$.
- **Car braking:** friction does negative work, so $ME$ decreases (it turns into thermal energy).
- **Elevator:** the motor does positive work, so $ME$ increases.
- **Skydiver:** the speed is constant, but the height decreases; air resistance dissipates the lost potential energy.
:::

:::question em-22
A pole vaulter runs, plants the pole, is lifted up and falls onto the mat. Order the dominant forms of energy in the sequence in which they appear.
---options---
- Kinetic energy of the athlete during the run-up
- Elastic potential energy of the bent pole
- Gravitational potential energy of the athlete at the highest point
- Kinetic energy of the athlete falling, just before touching the mat
---resolution---
1. During the **run-up**, the athlete builds up kinetic energy.
2. When the **pole is planted**, it bends and stores part of that energy as elastic potential energy.
3. The pole **straightens** and lifts the athlete, converting energy into gravitational potential energy, which is greatest at the highest point.
4. During the **fall**, the gravitational potential energy turns back into kinetic energy, which is dissipated by the mat.
:::
